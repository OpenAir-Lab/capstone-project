VIDEO 0 — “What Is OpenAir Lab?”
Rough Monologue Draft (unpolished, informational only)


“Welcome to OpenAir Lab. 

Our project is built around one simple idea: modern handheld radios have become black boxes. 
You buy a commercial transceiver, you press a few buttons, and everything inside is hidden. 
For students or new hobbyists, there’s no way to see how a radio actually works or how the different subsystems talk to each other.
OpenAir Lab is our answer to that problem. 
We designed a fully modular, open-source handheld radio platform where every major subsystem is separated into its own small ‘modulino,’ 
and everything plugs into the main microcontroller board through clearly defined connectors. 
The goal isn’t just to build a working radio, but to create a system you can learn from, modify, and hack on.

The platform is divided into several modules. 

The first is the MCU modulino, which uses an ESP32-WROVER-E. 
This board handles the digital logic, the communication buses, all the firmware, and the real-time task scheduling that keeps the radio 
responsive. 
Everything connects to it through an 18-pin EYESPI interface and a standard I²C expansion header.

Next is the RF modulino, built around the TI CC1200 transceiver. 
This is the actual radio front-end: it transmits and receives signals, measures RSSI, and supports packet-based digital communication. 
All interactions with the RF chip happen through SPI and dedicated interrupt lines, and students can see exactly what each register and pin does.

We also have the Audio modulino, which handles the microphone input and speaker output using digital audio interfaces. 
There’s a MEMS microphone that feeds data through I²S into the ESP32, and an amplifier that converts digital samples back into sound for the 
speaker. 
This lets students trace a real audio path through the system.

The HMI modulino is where users interact with the radio: the color TFT display, the keypad or buttons, the rotary encoder, and any indicator LEDs.
This module makes it easy to visualize state, navigate menus, tune frequencies, and monitor battery or signal strength.

And finally, the BMS and Power modulinos manages USB-PD input, battery charging, fuel gauging, and system power regulation. 
This gives students a complete look at how portable devices manage power safely and efficiently.

When you put all of these together, you get a fully functioning radio system, but more importantly, a platform designed for learning. 
Every cable, every connector, and every bus is exposed. 
Students can unplug individual pieces, probe signals, load example firmware, and complete structured labs that walk through each subsystem
in isolation before bringing them all together.

Throughout the project, we’ve created exercises that teach the fundamentals behind each part of the radio: how a microcontroller boots, how 
digital buses move data, how interrupts coordinate timing-sensitive events, how audio gets encoded and decoded, and how RF hardware is configured
and controlled.

The goal of OpenAir Lab is to make radio electronics approachable. 

Instead of treating the radio as a sealed device, we treat it as a kit that reveals how communication systems really work. 

By the end of the curriculum, students not only have a working radio, but a working understanding of everything that makes it possible.

That’s the heart of OpenAir Lab: a modular, transparent, hands-on learning platform that opens up the internals of modern radio design.”
